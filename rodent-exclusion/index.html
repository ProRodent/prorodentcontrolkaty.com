Why gaps keep opening.

Houses move. Wood swells in humid weather and shrinks in dry spells, door seals flatten, vent screens tear in wind and trim pulls away from siding. Each change can leave a gap that was not there last year.

A mouse can squeeze through a gap barely wider than a ballpoint pen, and a rat needs roughly half an inch. Those sizes surprise most owners, and they explain why animals come back after a hopeful patch.

Materials that last.

Rodents chew to keep their teeth worn down, so a barrier has to resist chewing, not just block a view. Sheet metal covers larger openings. Steel mesh packed into a gap stays put when sealant locks it in. A hard-setting sealant bonds the edges so nothing can be pulled out.

  • Sheet metal flashing for roof edges and wider gaps.
  • Stainless or galvanized mesh, packed tightly and sealed over.
  • Hard-setting sealant that bonds to siding, brick and slab.
  • Fine-gauge vent covers that still let air through.

Timing and sequence.

Sealing comes after trapping, once catches have slowed. If animals are still inside, they may be trapped against the structure. Where we cannot be sure, we leave a single exit point that lets animals leave but not return, then close it at the end.

Doubtful that every animal has left? Raise it with us. We may suggest trapping and removal first and schedule sealing for the visit after.

Keeping the work lasting.

A sealed house still needs upkeep. Trim limbs back from the roof, keep stored items off the walls, and check seals each spring and before storms. Mention anything you notice, such as new gnawing or a loose cover.

When damage was done before sealing, we price rodent damage repair as its own line, so the cost of sealing stays clear.

Prepare these answers.

  • Siding type and any visible gaps.
  • Vent styles on the roof, soffits and dryer.
  • How well the garage door meets the slab.
  • Trees or fences that touch the roofline.

First moves at home.

  • Close the door on the worst-affected room and leave it closed.
  • Dry pellets stay put; sweeping them lifts dust you do not want in your lungs.
  • Hardware-store bait can send a poisoned animal off to die in a duct or wall cavity.
  • Note the clock time when scratching starts, because timing says a lot about the species.

What to do first: CDC-based cleanup steps

Waste left by rodents may carry disease, which makes slow and wet the right way to work. The CDC cleanup page named at the end is the basis for each item.

  • Never dry-sweep, because a broom flings fine particles up to face height.
  • Air the space out first: open every door and window that opens, then give it thirty minutes.
  • Anything near droppings can carry unseen germs, which is why gloves go together with a real handwash.
  • Disinfect first, collect afterward, once the soaked waste has stood for about five minutes.
  • Tie the bag shut and put it with ordinary household trash. Lifting a dead rodent with bare fingers is never acceptable.
  • Feeling feverish or short of breath following exposure is a matter for a physician, so tell one about the cleanup.

Sealing questions, answered.

Why not use expanding foam?

Rodents chew through it easily, and some use it as nesting material. Foam can seal around metal, but it should not be the only barrier.

Can I seal gaps myself?

Small obvious gaps, yes, with metal mesh and sealant. Before you start, make sure no animal is trapped inside, and ask us about any gap larger than a half inch.

How do I know every gap is closed?

We cover the usual entries and check for sign of new activity on the return visit. No one can promise zero, but we aim to leave no obvious opening.

Does sealing change the look of the house?

The bulk of the work sits under trim, behind vents or low on a wall. Metal vent covers can be seen but are small.

Do you seal the roofline?

Yes, where it is safe to reach. Where the work needs a roofer, we say so and describe what to ask for.
