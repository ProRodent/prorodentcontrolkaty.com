Fences, mature trees and storm debris.

Trees shade roofs, and limbs that touch shingles give climbing rodents a bridge. If you have a mature tree near the house, tell us, and we look at where it meets the roof. Trimming is best left to a tree service, and we tell you which limbs matter.

After a strong storm, walk the foundation and look for debris against the wall, torn vent covers and new burrows. Piles of leaves and limbs create cover right at the house, and clearing them is quick.

The job in sequence.

A lot of calls start in the garage. Garage, basement and shed work checks door seals, wall penetrations and the stored goods, and then exclusion closes the room off. Whatever we find gets reported, minor items included, along with anything better suited to a different trade.

What each season brings.

Soggy leaves and limbs bank against foundations and conceal holes. Raking them away after a storm exposes what is going on and takes away cover.

Early signs in plain words.

Many people put off calling because a clue looks trivial. Take any of these as a reason to ask, not a reason to hold back.

  • Movement or noise in a wall you share with a neighbor.
  • Pellets hidden behind the range, fridge or dishwasher.
  • Tooth marks on a baseboard or the inside of a cabinet.
  • A next-door neighbor with the same problem.
  • Traps or bait you tried that did nothing.

Lots, walls and property lines.

Because the name covers a wide area, we ask about your own lot. Is it a house on a small lot or a larger property? Is there a fence, a shed or a mature tree touching the roof? Is there an open strip or a ditch behind it?

Parked vehicles and stored gear are easy to overlook. A truck, trailer or pile of totes can house animals for weeks, with the nest sitting in an engine bay or beneath a cover rather than inside the house.

What to do first.

  • Close the door on the worst-affected room and leave it closed.
  • Dry pellets stay put; sweeping them lifts dust you do not want in your lungs.
  • Hardware-store bait can send a poisoned animal off to die in a duct or wall cavity.
  • Note the clock time when scratching starts, because timing says a lot about the species.

Before you call: handling droppings safely

Waste left by rodents may carry disease, which makes slow and wet the right way to work. The CDC cleanup page named at the end is the basis for each item.

  • Never dry-sweep, because a broom flings fine particles up to face height.
  • A garage, shed or crawl space that has sat closed should get roughly half an hour of through-draft before you enter.
  • Since germs cannot be seen, begin with gloves and end with soap and water.
  • Wet everything through, allow around five minutes, then lift the waste with towels you plan to discard.
  • Combine wipes and debris in a single bag, knot it and discard it, covering your hands near any body.
  • Keep an eye on your health during the following weeks. If a fever or shortness of breath appears, give your doctor the exact details of what you touched.
