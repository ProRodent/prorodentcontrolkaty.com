What the animals leave behind.

Rodents nest in insulation, tunnel through it and use corners as toilets. Over weeks, the result is a layer of droppings and urine, torn batting and a smell that grows with heat. Even after the animals are gone, the waste remains.

Left in place, it keeps attracting new animals, stains ceilings and can carry germs into the air when disturbed. Cleanup removes the material and resets the space.

Why the order matters.

Cleanup is the fourth step of a rodent job, not the first. The animals have to be trapped out, the openings sealed and the catches confirmed before anyone scrubs. If you skip ahead, you may pay twice.

  • Confirm the traps have been empty for several nights.
  • Check that every entry the technician found is closed.
  • Plan removal and replacement of damaged insulation in one visit.
  • Schedule the work for the cooler hours in summer.

How the work is done safely.

We follow the same steps the CDC describes for homes. The space is aired out first. Waste is wetted with disinfectant so dust stays down, then wiped or bagged. Hands, tools and clothing are protected, and everything leaves the house in sealed bags.

Heat, humidity and timing.

Attics in this climate can become punishing in summer, so we plan for it. Early starts, short sessions and extra breaks are normal. In crawl spaces, humidity and standing damp can make smells linger, so we check for moisture and mention it if we find it.

Not every Katy house has a crawl space, and we cannot say which do. If you have one, tell us how you get in and how much room there is, since that affects how we work.

Snap a photo of the droppings or chew marks and mention it on the phone, which saves time.

After the cleanup.

A clean attic lets you see what is happening up there. If you notice new droppings, call quickly, because it means an entry was missed. Chewed ducts or wiring found during cleanup go into a repair estimate, and any remaining gaps go to exclusion work.

If the smell came before the droppings, a dead animal may be in the space. The odor removal page explains that search.

Say this on the call.

  • The way in to the attic or crawl space.
  • How much headroom the space has.
  • Where insulation looks matted, stained or missing.
  • Any smell, and whether it grows in the afternoon heat.

Quick steps for the wait.

  • If a pickup has nesting stuff under the hood, leave it parked until inspected.
  • Move lawn seed, kibble and bird food inside for the time being.
  • Save any gnawed item you find in a bag for the technician.
  • Find the main electrical panel in case a cable has been chewed.

Before we arrive: careful cleanup

Planning to clean before we come? Take the points in sequence and pause whenever one is unclear.

  • Keep compressed air, brooms and dry cloths out of any job involving droppings or soiled nesting.
  • In any shut-up space, fresh air comes ahead of every other step, and half an hour is the common guideline.
  • Single-use or reusable gloves both do the job, so long as the gloves and your hands are washed after.
  • Sequence counts: wet the waste, let it stand about five minutes, and only then wipe, which keeps dust from rising.
  • Close the bag, and keep skin well clear of the animal.
  • We offer no medical claims. If fever, muscle aches or labored breathing follow contact with waste, consult a physician.

Cleanup questions from Katy callers.

Why wait until the animals are gone?

Cleaning first means doing it twice. New droppings and nesting appear while entries stay open, and your money is spent on material that gets soiled again.

Is attic heat a problem for the work?

Yes. Summer attics get very hot, so cleanup is often scheduled for the cooler part of the day, and the crew takes breaks. We tell you the expected timing when we book.

Do you replace insulation?

We can. Insulation that is stained, flattened or full of droppings should come out. We price removal and replacement as separate lines.

Is cleaning dangerous?

Dry droppings and nests can carry germs, so we air out the space, wet the waste, wear protection and bag everything. The method comes from the CDC pages listed at the foot of this page.

Can I clean it myself?

Small amounts, yes, if you follow the CDC method and wear gloves and a mask. A large attic needs professional gear.
