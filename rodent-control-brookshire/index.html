How the work unfolds.

A nest beneath the hood of a parked truck is a matter for rodents in cars, trucks and trailers, and a house inspection can ride along on the same trip. No price is named until we have viewed the property, and the choice of option stays with you.

Vehicles, trailers and stored equipment.

A truck, a trailer or a stack of equipment that stays parked for weeks is a favorite site for a nest. Check under the hood and around the wheel wells before you drive, and look for shredded material near belts or wiring.

If you store tools or feed in a shed, lift containers off the floor and keep lids tight. We look at those spaces during the walk-through, since animals often settle there first and then work toward the house.

Is this what you are seeing?

Hardly anyone starts a call with a grand statement. It begins with a rustle, a whiff or a single stray dropping. Such small things are what we hear first, and the walk-through builds a plan from them.

  • Movement or noise in a wall you share with a neighbor.
  • Pellets hidden behind the range, fridge or dishwasher.
  • Tooth marks on a baseboard or the inside of a cabinet.
  • A next-door neighbor with the same problem.
  • Traps or bait you tried that did nothing.

Hot afternoons drive animals toward shade and cool air. Garages, soffits and the area under a deck look attractive, and a brief walk outdoors before the hottest weeks costs almost nothing.

Preparing for the technician.

A town can hold houses, shops and outbuildings in any mix. We ask about the building in front of us. Is it a house, a shop or a storage structure? Does it have a slab or a raised floor? Is the roof shaded by trees?

Rats like to travel along borders: overgrown brush, a low wall, a fence run, the line where grass meets a drainage ditch. Those borders act as highways, which is why a lot beside one often has more traffic near its foundation.

Safety guidance built on CDC advice

Going slowly at the start keeps fine dust out of your airways.

  • No matter how small the mess, skip dry methods and start with a disinfectant solution.
  • Prop windows and doors open, stay outdoors for about thirty minutes, and let the air turn over.
  • Gloves guard you while you work, and soap guards you once you stop.
  • Leave droppings alone until they are drenched and have sat for around five minutes.
  • Close the bag, and keep skin well clear of the animal.
  • Sick a few weeks afterward? Tell a clinician about the rodent contact.
