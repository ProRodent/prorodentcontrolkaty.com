What the yard does for rodents.

A rodent needs three things: food, water and cover. A tidy yard can still supply them. Fallen fruit, pet bowls, bird seed, a dripping hose and a stack of lumber are all common, and none require neglect.

Katy lots vary widely, from compact fenced yards to wide lots. What matters is how the yard is arranged relative to the house, and whether a clear path leads from cover to a wall.

A walk around the lot.

A yard inspection follows the animals' logic. We look at the fence line and the base of any shed, then at plantings against the siding, then at any tree or cable that reaches the roof. Burrows and rub marks tell us which route is active.

Edges, corridors and nearby green space.

Rodents like edges. A fence line, a bank of tall grass or a strip of trees creates a corridor they can travel without crossing open ground. If your lot backs onto one, tell us which side. Reference points such as VFW Park or a drainage easement help us picture that edge, though we do not know their layout beyond the name.

We cannot change what lies beyond your fence. We can make sure your side offers less food and no easy path to the house, and we can seal the house itself so a visitor has nowhere to go.

Simple changes that pay off.

  • Pick up fallen fruit every day or two during the season.
  • Store pet food and seed in metal or hard plastic with tight lids.
  • Keep stacked items several inches off the ground and away from walls.
  • Trim limbs and vines so none touches the roof or siding.
  • Fix leaking hoses and spigots that make a steady water source.

None of these is dramatic. Together they cut the draw, and most take an afternoon.

When we set traps outdoors.

Where a burrow is active or a runway is clear, we place traps in protected stations and check them on a schedule. We do not scatter bait in open yards, since pets, wildlife and children can reach it. The trapping page explains how stations work.

Once the yard is under control, the house still needs sealing. See entry point sealing for the next step, or rat control if the main trouble is overhead.

What the dispatcher asks.

  • Where you saw burrows, droppings or movement.
  • Fruit trees, feeders, compost or pet food outdoors.
  • Fence type and any gaps beneath it.
  • Sheds, play structures or stacked wood near the house.

Small tasks before the visit.

  • Check that the side gate swings open, and let us know if a dog runs loose in the yard.
  • Leave shrubs and vines touching the walls alone until they have been seen.
  • Put a flashlight somewhere handy for shadowy corners.
  • Take photos of burrows, pellets and nest scraps before anything is covered over or moved.

Before we arrive: careful cleanup

Agencies broadly agree on how to clean up, and the overlap is condensed here.

  • No matter how small the mess, skip dry methods and start with a disinfectant solution.
  • Do not step straight into a locked outbuilding. Let a breeze run through for around half an hour.
  • Single-use or reusable gloves both do the job, so long as the gloves and your hands are washed after.
  • Drench each pellet and stain in disinfectant, and give it roughly five minutes before touching any of it.
  • One securely closed bag is enough to carry droppings, towels and a carcass outside.
  • Keep an eye on your health during the following weeks. If a fever or shortness of breath appears, give your doctor the exact details of what you touched.

Yard questions from Katy callers.

Do fruit trees attract rats?

Fallen fruit does. Picking it up often and keeping the ground clear removes a food source without losing the tree.

Does a feeder cause a problem?

Spilled seed can feed rodents at night. A tray under the feeder, a tight lid on storage and a cleanup each evening reduce that.

What about a drainage ditch behind the lot?

Ditches and fence lines can serve as travel lanes. We cannot change them, but we can seal the house and reduce food nearby.

How do I find a burrow?

Look for holes about two inches across beside a slab, shed or fence, often with smooth dirt around them. Heavy rain can open or hide them.

Do you treat the entire yard?

We focus on the zones that connect to the house: foundation, fence, trees and sheds. Treating every square foot is not practical or necessary.
