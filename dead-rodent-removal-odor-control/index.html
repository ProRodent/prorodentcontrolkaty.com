What that smell usually means.

A dead rodent smells sharp at first, then heavy and sweet. In an air-conditioned house in summer, the smell can move through ducts and gaps, so the strongest room may not be the nearest to the animal.

Not every bad smell is a rodent. Sewer gas, a wet carpet, a spoiled item behind an appliance and mold can all smell similar. We rule those out as we search.

How we trace the source.

  • Move through each room and mark where the odor peaks and where it thins out.
  • Check vents, returns and the air handler for drifting odor.
  • Look for flies, stains or small bumps on a ceiling or wall.
  • Inspect the attic, any under-floor space and the garage.
  • Use a mirror and flashlight at gaps, not a hammer.

If the animal is in a wall cavity, we look for the least damaging way in, such as an access panel or the back of a cabinet. We tell you before we cut anything.

The CDC steps below describe how to handle a carcass safely if you must do it yourself. We recommend leaving it to us when it is inside a wall, duct or attic.

While you wait.

  • Open windows and run fans if the weather allows.
  • Send kids and pets out of the smelliest room.
  • Do not spray air fresheners at a wall, which only mask the smell.
  • Pass on store poison, which can leave more animals in hidden spaces.
  • Photograph any stains or flies for the technician.

Preventing a repeat.

A dead animal means one entry was open. We find it and close it with exclusion work. If poison caused the death, we talk about alternatives, because poisoned animals can end up in walls again. The trapping page describes the approach we prefer.

If the smell has stained insulation or drywall, attic and crawl space decontamination and damage repair finish the job.

Prepare these answers.

  • The room where the odor is worst.
  • Whether flies have appeared near a vent or ceiling.
  • When the smell began and whether it is growing.
  • Whether the air conditioning seems to spread it.

Before the crew shows up.

  • If a pickup has nesting stuff under the hood, leave it parked until inspected.
  • Move lawn seed, kibble and bird food inside for the time being.
  • Save any gnawed item you find in a bag for the technician.
  • Find the main electrical panel in case a cable has been chewed.

What to do first: CDC-based cleanup steps

Planning to clean before we come? Take the points in sequence and pause whenever one is unclear.

  • Keep compressed air, brooms and dry cloths out of any job involving droppings or soiled nesting.
  • A room sealed for weeks needs thirty minutes of outside air.
  • Since germs cannot be seen, begin with gloves and end with soap and water.
  • Leave droppings alone until they are drenched and have sat for around five minutes.
  • Choose a bag that seals firmly, and use gloves or a tool as a barrier against the carcass.
  • Medical questions go to medical staff, and any symptom is a reason to describe to them what happened.

Odor questions from Katy callers.

How many days will the odor linger?

Usually one to two weeks, though body size, warmth and ventilation in the space all play a part. Removing the carcass and treating the area shortens the wait.

Why does it smell worse in the afternoon?

Heat speeds decay and moves air through vents and gaps. In a hot attic or wall, odors can spread faster.

Can air fresheners fix it?

They mask a smell for a while but do nothing about the source. Removing the animal is the only real fix.

Is it safe to stay in the house?

Usually yes, but keep children and pets away from the strongest room and ventilate. If anyone feels unwell, see a doctor.

Will you cut into a wall?

Only when needed. We try access points such as hatches, vents and ceiling edges first, then tell you before any cutting.
