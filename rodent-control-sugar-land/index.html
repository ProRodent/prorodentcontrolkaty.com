Picturing your lot.

Because the city is big, we do not describe a typical property. We ask about yours. House or townhouse? Does the garage face the street? Do trees touch the roof? Is there a shared wall or a fence line that borders open ground?

Rats like to travel along borders: overgrown brush, a low wall, a fence run, the line where grass meets a drainage ditch. Those borders act as highways, which is why a lot beside one often has more traffic near its foundation.

Early signs in plain words.

Hardly anyone starts a call with a grand statement. It begins with a rustle, a whiff or a single stray dropping. Such small things are what we hear first, and the walk-through builds a plan from them.

  • Movement or noise in a wall you share with a neighbor.
  • Pellets hidden behind the range, fridge or dishwasher.
  • Tooth marks on a baseboard or the inside of a cabinet.
  • A next-door neighbor with the same problem.
  • Traps or bait you tried that did nothing.

Weather and rodent behavior.

Before you call: handling droppings safely

Everything here repeats guidance from public health bodies, so any household can use it whether or not it ever books a service.

  • Dampen first, wipe second, and never sweep dry.
  • In any shut-up space, fresh air comes ahead of every other step, and half an hour is the common guideline.
  • Put on rubber, vinyl or comparable gloves for soiled items, and afterward lather your hands with soap and warm water.
  • Saturate droppings along with the spot below them using household disinfectant or a label-strength bleach solution. Leave it for about five minutes before blotting with paper towels.
  • One securely closed bag is enough to carry droppings, towels and a carcass outside.
  • Keep an eye on your health during the following weeks. If a fever or shortness of breath appears, give your doctor the exact details of what you touched.
